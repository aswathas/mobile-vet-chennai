# Locality Dossier: where the mobile veterinary service should actually sit

## Verdict

**Open the first route in Zone A, on the Velachery cluster. Do not open the OMR corridor until that route is already doing six visits a day.**

Here is why, in one paragraph. Zone A holds 11,576 of the 16,583 cases a month that the two corridors hold, and Rs 1.51 crore of the Rs 2.24 crore that our service mix could earn in them. Its veterinary competition is much thinner: mean saturation 31.8 against Zone B's 59.6. Hold the Thiruvanmiyur cluster for the third route, not the second. It earns Rs 91,728 of contribution per route against the Velachery route's Rs 70,668, because the OMR price band is about Rs 150 higher. It is also the most contested ground in the corpus, at a saturation index of 63.

## How this dossier was built

The market report next to this one says a house-call veterinary practice in Chennai should launch. It names three candidate clusters and does not choose between them. This dossier chooses, at the level where the choice is actually made: one hundred named localities, each with a ward, a household count, a pet count, a demand curve by hour of day, a demand split by day of the week, a demand curve by month, the price households there pay, the revenue one route could reach there, and the veterinary supply already on the ground.

Two research passes built it. One walked the south-east cluster. One walked the OMR and ECR corridor. Each collected its own census, property-tax, street inventory, operator-price and mapping evidence, and each published its own model note. Then the two were put onto one common basis. Three corrections were needed, and they are written down here rather than buried, because the first version of this dossier was wrong in a way that would have changed the recommendation.

Three numbers decide everything, and they are the same three the market report used, carried down to the level of a single locality.

The diagram above is the whole chain in one line, from households to the revenue a single route can reach. Read it once and the rest of the dossier is its commentary.

- **Cost per visit.** A clustered Chennai round trip costs about Rs 850 to deliver: a veterinarian and a para-vet in one vehicle, fuel, depreciation, insurance, the base premises, consumables.
- **The floor.** A blended price under Rs 1,100 does not carry a visit. Blended means the average price of everything sold on that route, not the headline price of one service.
- **The ceiling.** Six visits a day, about 156 a month, is what one vehicle can physically do in Chennai traffic.

### Where the numbers come from, and how far to trust them

Every locality figure carries a tier, and the tier legend sits in the margin beside this line. Tier A means a page or a document was actually opened and read, and the source is listed. Tier B means arithmetic on tier A inputs, with the formula in the model note. Tier C means a modelled assumption, with its band stated.


Two honest limits. First, no figure here is tier A. The corpus is built from public sources, but the case counts are built from them, and in half the corpus from a modelled household base. The OMR corridor's households come from the Census and the Corporation's property-tax ledger, so its quantities are tier B. The south-east cluster's households come from a modelled built-footprint density, so its quantities are tier C. The case rate itself is modelled in both. What the tiers buy is that the reader knows exactly which numbers would survive a challenge.

Second, the comparison between localities is much more trustworthy than the absolute level of any one of them. When one locality outranks another by a factor of three, that ranking survives the uncertainty in the model. When the model says a locality holds 838 cases a month, that number is an estimate and not a measurement.

### What "peak cover" means here

Peak cover is the maximum plausible number of cases in one locality on one day at the seasonal peak. It is demand, not capacity, and it is the number that should decide whether a locality needs a stop of its own on a fixed weekly round or is a drive-by. Across the corpus the peak cover comes to 1,225 cases a day. One locality, Velachery main, can absorb 51 of them. One, R.M.Z. Ecoworld in Thandalam, can absorb 1.

### The go, hold and no-go rule

Every locality carries a verdict, and the rule is the same everywhere, so no locality is graded generously.

- **GO** needs all four: our blended price at or above Rs 1,100, a margin over the Rs 850 clustered cost of at least Rs 250, at least 40 cases a month, and a competition saturation index under 70.
- **HOLD** is at least 15 cases a month with one of the four failing.
- **NO-GO** is fewer than 15 cases a month, or a margin under Rs 100.

Rank is prize. The order runs by our-mix monthly revenue, not by a composite score.

Eighty-two localities are GO, sixteen are HOLD and two are NO-GO. Three HOLDs are in Zone A and thirteen in Zone B, and every Zone B HOLD fails on one thing only. Eight of them fail on saturation: the four Navalur localities sit at 71 because the Little Angels home-visit arm is physically based there, and Thazhuthendur, Kandanchal, Kovilambedu and Egumudi sit at 67 for the same reason on the same corridor. The other five fail on volume: E.L.C.O.T., the Infosys campus township, Enjambakkam, Enjambakkam Cholan Nagar and Sungudi East Coast Road hold 21 to 38 cases a month. That is a precise, fixable problem, and it is the whole of the difference between the two zones.

### The corrections this dossier had to make to its own research

The first pass compared two models that did not measure the same thing, and the comparison was wrong in the direction that flatters the second zone. Three fixes were applied.

**A unit mismatch.** One pass measured veterinary demand per household a year. The other measured it per pet-owning household a year. Set side by side, they made the OMR corridor look six times larger than the south-east cluster. That was an artefact of the units, not a fact about the corridor. Everything in this dossier is now measured on one city-level calibration: the Greater Chennai Corporation's own working figures from the August to October 2026 vaccination campaign, 1,50,303 dogs vaccinated against a 2,00,000 target, spread over the 1,733,423 households the 2011 Census and the Corporation's property-tax growth give, with veterinary demand at 4.0 contacts per pet-owning household a year.

**A price-basis mismatch.** One pass measured the blended price a route earns. The other measured the price a single locality's demand would fetch. The first is what the market report says decides a route. The second is a different question, and it flattered or punished localities for reasons that have nothing to do with where to launch. Every locality here now carries the blended price of the route it actually rides, and the locality's own fill-diluted number is kept beside it as a separate field.

**A tier overstatement.** The first harmonisation stamped every figure as tier B, including the south-east cluster, whose household base is modelled rather than sourced. The tiers are now honest: 50 localities tier B, 50 tier C, none tier A.

None of the three corrections changes the recommendation. All three would have changed it if left alone, which is the reason they are written down here.

## Zone A: the south-east cluster

Zone A is the larger of the two on every physical measure. Fifty localities, 271,686 households, 34,728 pet-owning households, which is 12.8 percent of all households, the highest pet-ownership rate of the two corridors. It holds 11,576 cases a month, 386 on an ordinary weekday, and at the route-cluster blended prices it carries Rs 1,50,82,166 a month for our mix against Rs 1,09,56,703 for the whole market at its own prices. Our mix earns more than the market here, because the market's median visit is Rs 951 and our mix sells chronic monitoring, senior wellness and end-of-life care.

Three things make this the better first corridor. One makes it worse.

The competition is the thinnest in the corpus, at a mean saturation index of 31.8 against Zone B's 59.6. In Zone B, Little Angels at Navallur, Vetic, EASZO, ThePetNest, Sanchu and the Corporation's own free centres all sit on the same corridor. In Zone A the named operators are spread out: Dr P. Ramadass at Medavakkam at Rs 200, Vet At Home at Perungalathur, Sanchu at Velachery, Blue Cross at Velachery, and a handful of clinic home-visit arms.

The demand shape is better. Zone A's weekend lift is the strongest in the corpus, Saturday at 18.0 percent and Sunday at 18.6 percent against a 12.2 to 12.6 percent Monday to Thursday, and it is the shape a house-call business wants, because owners are home at the weekend. Zone B's Sunday is its weakest day at 12.9 percent, because the OMR workforce is at work.

The season is flatter. Zone A's demand index runs from 117.8 in January to 85.0 in August, a 32-point band. Zone B runs from 121.2 to 79.7, a 41-point band, because the southwest monsoon lands harder on an exposed east-west corridor than on the inland south-east cluster.

The bad thing is the price. Zone A's median market price is Rs 951 against Zone B's Rs 932, which sounds level, and it is, but Zone A's cluster blends run between Rs 1,295 and Rs 1,311 while Zone B's run from Rs 1,384 to Rs 1,557. The south-east corridor cannot charge what the OMR corridor can, and the difference lands on the route: Rs 1,701 of revenue per route-hour against Rs 1,878.

## Zone B: the OMR and ECR corridor

Zone B is the commercially sharper corridor and the harder one to win. Fifty localities, 147,113 households, 15,017 pet-owning households at 10.2 percent, and 5,007 cases a month. Its cluster blends run Rs 1,384 to Rs 1,557, the highest in the corpus, and one route in the Thiruvanmiyur cluster earns Rs 91,728 of contribution against Rs 70,668 in the best Velachery route.

That difference is the entire case for Zone B, and it is worth Rs 21,000 a route a month. It is also the only case for it.

The corridor is crowded. Mean saturation is 59.6, and thirteen of Zone B's fifty localities are HOLD, eight of them on saturation alone. Navalur's four localities sit at 71 because the Little Angels home-visit arm is based there. Thazhuthendur, Kandanchal, Kovilambedu and Egumudi sit at 67. Enjambakkam and Sungudi East Coast Road sit at 58. A corridor where a quarter of its localities already fail on saturation is a corridor where a new operator is not the first in the field.

The demand is thinner. Zone B holds 5,007 cases a month across 147,113 households, against Zone A's 11,576 across 271,686, and the two zones together hold 16,583. Its peak cover is lower at every rank: its best locality, Thiruvanmiyur V.O.C. Street, reaches 27 against Velachery main's 51.

Its season is worse. July is its worst month at 79.7 against Zone A's 85.0 in August, and the monsoon lands on a long, exposed, east-west corridor.

And the money sits in one place. Zone B's top ten localities hold most of its value, and they are not spread along the OMR. They are Thiruvanmiyur, four localities of it, Perungudi and Palavakkam. The OMR spine itself, Navalur through Thazhuthendur, is four HOLD localities and a thin cluster.

## The shape of a Chennai pet day

Demand has a shape, and the shape is the same in both corridors: a morning band, a lunch dip, a second band, and a long thin evening tail.

{{chart:hour-curve}}

Zone A peaks at 10:00 with 8.1 percent of the day's demand, then 11:00 at 7.5 and 09:00 at 6.9. Zone B peaks an hour later, at 11:00 with 10.7 percent, and its 15:00 hour carries almost as much as its 10:00 hour, 9.4 against 9.4. That difference is a working-hours difference. The south-east cluster's pet owners are home before the corridor's workers leave for it. The OMR's are in their air-conditioned offices until the evening.

The bookable window should therefore be 10:00 to 16:00 in Zone A and 10:00 to 16:30 in Zone B, and nothing outside it. The market report's corridor study put the worst congestion between 2:45 and 4:15 pm, and a route that runs to 4:30 in a Chennai afternoon loses the visit to the traffic.

### The staffing plan that falls out of it

{{table:staffing-plan}}

The staffing plan is one veterinarian and one para-vet per route, on a fixed weekly round, with a second pair for the two clusters that carry more than one route's worth of demand. The peak-hour column is the reason the round is fixed rather than booked. Demand in the 10:00 hour is 8 to 11 percent of the day in both corridors, so a route that starts late loses its best hours, and a route that starts early in summer loses them to the heat.

Seasonally, January is the best month in both corridors, and the Corporation's mass vaccination campaign window is the worst thing that can happen to a launch month. The campaign of August to October 2026 vaccinated 1,50,303 dogs against a 2,00,000 target. Those are free vaccinations at eleven centres. A launch that lands in that window sells licences and vaccination into free supply. A launch three months before it turns the campaign into an acquisition channel instead.

## Where the money is

The corpus does not spread evenly, and it is not supposed to. Twenty localities hold 46.6 percent of the our-mix revenue, and the top ten hold 28.7 percent. The other eighty are not worthless, but they are stops inside a round, not rounds. That difference decides how the route is designed: four localities in Velachery, three in Medavakkam and three in Pallikaranai are worth building around, and the rest of the eighty are the reasons a route can pay for itself.

This is also where capacity bites. One route is 156 visits a month. Fifteen clusters at one route each reach 2,340 visits, which is 14 percent of the 16,583 cases a month the two corridors hold. The corpus is roughly seven times larger than the fleet that could serve it. **The binding constraint on this business is not the size of the market. It is the number of visits a team can sell, staff and deliver in a day.** A dossier that only counted demand would have told you the market is enormous and stopped there, which would have been the wrong answer.

Fifteen clusters, eight in Zone A and seven in Zone B, each a contiguous round a single vehicle can serve. Every one of them carries more demand than a single route can reach, and ten of them carry more than 800 cases a month: A-R7 at 2,600, A-R5 at 2,473, A-R6 at 1,968, B-R7 at 1,323, A-R3 at 1,145, A-R8 at 995, A-R1 at 908, A-R4 at 895, B-R5 at 882 and B-R4 at 858. The two thinnest, B-R2 Thazhuthendur at 326 and B-R3 Sholinganallur at 344, are the ones that would first justify a second vehicle on the same corridor.

The order should be this.

**Route one, A-R7, anchored on Velachery main.** It holds the most demand in the corpus, 2,600 cases a month across seven localities, the highest peak cover in the corpus at 51 cases in a day, and it sits on the corridor's own hub. Three named operators already work inside Velachery, which means the demand is observed rather than inferred. One route earns Rs 70,668 a month of contribution.

**Route two, A-R5, anchored on Medavakkam Town.** 2,473 cases across eight localities, the lowest saturation among the big clusters at 36, and the airport corridor's new households on its western edge. One route earns Rs 70,824.

**Route three, B-R7, anchored on Thiruvanmiyur.** This is the best route in the corpus on money and the worst on competition. It earns Rs 91,728 of contribution, the highest in the corpus, and it sits at a saturation index of 63. Open it once the first two routes are carrying their full six visits a day, because it is a route that can be filled from day one but not defended from day one.

**Never route to the OMR spine as it stands.** Navalur, Thazhuthendur, Kandanchal, Kovilambedu and Egumudi are five HOLD localities on one line, all failing on saturation. They are worth a stop inside a round that runs elsewhere, not a round of their own.

### What a day costs in minutes

The number that decides whether a cluster works is not cases. It is minutes. The market report's cost model puts clinical work at about 25 minutes a visit. Travel is the variable: a clustered round averages roughly 6.7 km and 21 minutes of travel per visit, and a scattered one averages 12 km and 37 minutes. That difference, 16 minutes a visit, is the whole distance between the Rs 850 clustered cost and a cost that breaks the Rs 1,100 floor on its own.

Which is why the route plan above is a list of clusters and not a list of localities. A route that serves one Velachery cluster properly, four to eight stops inside fifteen minutes of each other, spends about 21 minutes travelling per visit and arrives at a visit that costs Rs 850. A route that crosses the corridor to pick up a thirteenth stop spends 37 minutes travelling for the same 25 minutes of work, and the visit that used to be worth Rs 500 of contribution is worth less than nothing.

Two of the corpus's own clusters are stretched and say so. B-R4 runs ten localities from Palavakkam and is the widest round in the corpus. B-R2 runs eight from Thazhuthendur down the OMR spine. Both are flagged in the data as clustered but carrying 21 minutes between stops, which is the threshold above which a cluster stops being a cluster.

## What would change the verdict

This dossier is a plan for where to look, not a promise. Four things would move it, and they are worth watching from week one.

**A price floor that does not hold.** Every GO locality here clears Rs 1,100 because that is the condition attached. If a launch cluster's actual blended price lands under it, the cluster becomes a HOLD no matter what the demand column says, because the route loses about Rs 250 on every visit it makes there. The first three months of bookings will tell the team whether owners pay Rs 1,199 for an extended consultation with a written plan, or whether they treat a house call as a Rs 600 errand. Zone A's market price of Rs 951 is the number that should worry the founding team, and it is why the pilot should discount the plan and never the visit.

**A partner clinic that does not get signed.** The market report made a signed partner clinic a month-three gate. Route-only economics have no facility to absorb a slow month, a heat wave, or a week when the veterinarian is ill. A locality next to a clinic that will take procedures and share records is worth more than a bigger locality with no fallback, and the cluster table shows where that is available.

**Route density that does not materialise.** Four billable services per trip is what makes a clustered visit cost Rs 850 rather than about Rs 1,050. Every locality was given a route cluster, and every cluster was checked for whether one vehicle can serve it without scattering. If the bookings do not fill a cluster's stops, the cost per visit rises and the margin with it, and the fix is fewer localities rather than more bookings.

**A licence regime that tightens or loosens.** The Corporation registry is the acquisition engine, not a profit line. The two corridors differ sharply here: Zone A's pet base is 33.5 percent licensed against Zone B's 21.3 percent, so the doorstep licence-compliance bundle has a longer sale in Zone B and a shorter one in Zone A. Free microchipping, free vaccination and free licence guidance at eleven centres mean the doorstep service is worth money because the Corporation does not come to the house. A campaign window turns acquisition months into revenue months by accident. Launching into a campaign is a waste of the season.

And one thing this dossier cannot tell you at all. It models demand, not willingness to pay at these prices, and it models it from a corridor of households rather than from a sales pipeline. The tier marks say which numbers would survive a challenge and which would not. The ranking between localities is the durable part. The absolute case counts are the soft part, and any one locality's case count should be treated as a hypothesis to test in week one, not a finding to bank.
