# The problem

The founding team of a mobile veterinary house-call practice in Chennai had a
market report and no place to stand. The report told them to launch in Chennai,
above a blended price of Rs 1,100 a visit, restricted to chronic, senior and
end-of-life work. It named three candidate clusters — a south-east cluster, an
OMR and Navallur cluster, and a north-central one — and stopped there.

The decision they still had to make was the expensive one: which street to put
the vehicle on.

Their own words, at the start:

> "I want to investigate the whole Chennai and surrounding area. The places where
> I can put my mobile vet service."

> "I want a place wise analysis for each place. How many customers might be
> there — not only licensed, because a lot of people are unlicensed, so we call
> them pet customers."

> "I want an hour-wise report on which hours will be the medium, and also week
> wise comparison of which weekdays or weekends will be high in cases number.
> Also what is an average cost, what is the average price that the customers
> make, also what will be a monthly analysis, also an average earnings per day,
> weekday wise, hourly."

> "I want it very comprehensive. I want a simple English, and the people who
> read this will be veterinarians."

And then the sentence that changed the shape of the deliverable:

> "I want a conclusive report, because we are humans. People won't look at
> charts and things."

Two things follow from that last line, and they pull against each other. A
report a partner will read once, carefully, and then argue about in a room has
to carry its argument in sentences. But the reader is a veterinarian, so the
numbers can be dense, the clinical vocabulary can stay as it is, and the
commercial terms have to be defined rather than assumed. The business terms —
blended price, margin, contribution, saturation — are the ones that need
explaining. The clinical ones do not.

What was missing, concretely: a hundred named localities with demand figures,
an hour-of-day and day-of-week shape, a price band, a monthly figure, a
per-route earning, a ranked go or no-go, and a route plan that survives the
question "and if the rains come in June?"

What it costs them to stay where they are: a route that cannot fill six visits
a day loses about Rs 250 on every visit it makes, because the cost of a
clustered Chennai round trip is about Rs 850 and the market floor is around
Rs 600. Every month without a chosen corridor is a month of capacity spent
against an unknown route.

And if it is never fixed: the practice launches into a corridor it picked by
reputation, discovers in month three that the price band there will not carry a
visit, and restructures under pressure with a team already on the road.
