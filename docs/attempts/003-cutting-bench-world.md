# Attempt 003 — Keeping the cutting bench

**Hypothesis.** The world that already shipped — a film cutting bench, a
perforated rail, the sources hung as trims on pins — could carry a hundred
localities and fifteen route clusters as well as it carried ten tables and
eighty-three sources. The corpus is the same report, longer.

**How we tested it.** The world was put to the test honestly: the corpus was
rendered inside it, the decision board was drawn in its grammar, the
hundred-row appendix was set in its punched windows, and the result was
reviewed.

**What actually happened.** The frame did not scale. Every chart, curve and
table had to live inside a punched white window with a perforated left edge,
so the appendix became a hundred punched windows rather than a register. A
condensed grotesk at seventy-eight percent stretch under coarse grain will not
hold thirteen-pixel tabular numerals across six hundred competitor rows. And
the black ground means every figure needs an inverted window, which is a great
way to make a document feel like a gallery of artefacts rather than a page.

There was a second cost. The incumbent world was the strongest thing in the
roll: it identified the reader better than any of the eleven alternatives. But
a replacement that keeps the incumbent's ground is a lateral move, and the
whole point of replacing a world is to stop doing what the corpus outgrew.

**Outcome.** Abandoned as the final world. Its best idea survived as a raise:
sources as countable physical objects hung from the page, which is now the
clipped docket on the dossier's margin.

**Why we moved on.** A world is chosen for the corpus it has to carry. Ten
tables and eighty-three sources fit inside a film strip. A hundred localities,
fifteen route clusters and six hundred competitor rows do not, and pretending
otherwise produced a page that was beautiful and unreadable at exactly the
place the reader needs to work.
